# ProtKiosk: what went wrong and how I would start again

ProtKiosk was failing within two hours of opening. I had a kiosk at the gym entrance, a six-month contract with a caterer and a 40-page business plan, and on the first day almost none of it worked the way I planned. When I talked to my mentor about it, he told me the main problem was that I treated an uncertain venture like a predictable one. After going through Module 1, I agree with him. In this essay I go through my mistakes, how I would start again, and how I should have paid for it.

## Part 1: Analyzing my mistakes

It did not take long to see that I believed most of the myths the course talks about. The first one was the big idea: I spent four months alone on a 40-page plan and did not talk to one customer. I also believed that entrepreneurs are big risk-takers who go all in, so I put €10,000, all my savings, into the kiosk and signed a six-month contract with a caterer before I had sold a single meal. And because I thought you need a lot of money to start, I asked a VC for €2 million. Behind all of this was the idea that passion is enough. I was sure there was a "massive untapped market" and never checked if that was true.

The course separates situations into predictable, risky and uncertain. In a predictable situation you know what the outcomes will be. In a risky one you do not know the outcome but you know the odds, like rolling a dice. When it is uncertain you know neither. ProtKiosk was uncertain: I did not know if people at the gym wanted a meal right after training, how much they would pay, or what the gym's rules were. I still used tools that only work when things are predictable, like a long plan, a big fixed investment and a 200% growth forecast. Only after opening did I find out about the gym's exclusivity contract. My €12 price also turned out to be too high for most people, and I had to throw away food that went bad.

When I looked at the business model, I had not tested any part of it. My value proposition, my customer segment, my channel (the gym entrance) and my cost structure (the fixed caterer contract) were all guesses that I wrote down as if they were facts.

## Part 2: The "gym reality" pivot

The biggest thing I would change is moving from execution mode to search mode. I treated the startup as a plan to follow, when it should have been a search for a business model that works. In practice that means observing first, then making a hypothesis, and then testing it cheaply.

So I started by observing. I went to Anytime Fitness for the first time on 20 August 2025. It was close to noon and the place was calm, with barely anyone there. People were taking turns on the pec deck machine, a couple were on the treadmills, and some were squatting a lot of weight. Roughly 8 to 10 people left while I was there. After my workout I went to the Subway nearby and saw two of the guys from the pec deck machine. I asked them for advice. They told me not to buy protein shakes and pre-workout from the gym's vending machine and to buy them outside instead. They also said the gym mainly sells memberships and gives discounts to its members.

It was a small sample from a quiet day, so it is a starting point and not proof, but it gave me two hypotheses. First, people do spend money on food and supplements after training, just not inside the gym, and they don't mind going somewhere else to get it. Second, the gym makes its money from memberships, so it has more reason to work with a food seller than to compete with one.

Effectuation helps me decide what to do with this. Bird-in-hand means I start from who I am (someone who trains), what I know (what people eat after a workout) and who I know (gym friends, campus clubs and a local café). Under affordable loss I decide before I start that the most I can lose is €150, not €10,000. The crazy quilt principle has me looking for partners who will commit early, for example by offering the gym's members a discount, which fits with what the gym already does. Lemonade turns the exclusivity contract from a dead end into a reason to sell outside the gym through pre-orders. And pilot-in-the-plane means I build the opportunity with the people around me instead of predicting it from my desk.

My MVP would be 20 pre-ordered protein meals for one week, ordered through WhatsApp and Instagram and picked up near the gym. I would measure how many people pre-order, how many order again, and what price they are okay with compared to €12. If at least a quarter of the first-week buyers order again at a price that covers my costs, I keep going. If not, I pivot, maybe to supplying a café near the gym instead of selling directly.

## Part 3: Financing

Going to a DeepTech/software VC was the wrong choice. VCs invest in technology that can scale and give them huge returns, and a food kiosk is low-tech, local and depends on physical stock and equipment. I also had no customers and no tested model, so to them ProtKiosk was far too risky.

I could bootstrap within my affordable loss to keep the risk small, and resell or reuse the kiosk to get back part of the €10,000. There is also money I would not have to give up equity for, like a school incubator grant, a student entrepreneurship competition or crowdfunding. A revenue share with the gym or a sports club is another option.

For me the best option is customer funding through paid pre-orders or weekly subscriptions. The customer pays before I buy the ingredients, so money comes in before costs go out and no food goes to waste. Every pre-order is also evidence that people want the product, which is exactly what I did not have the first time.

## Conclusion

The main thing I learned is that when I don't know the outcomes or the odds, I should take small steps and learn from each one instead of writing a big plan and betting everything on it. The course is clear that planning has its place: there is a time to wear the entrepreneurial hat and a time to wear the manager's hat, and when you know the outcomes and the odds, planning works. ProtKiosk was not that situation, so I should have run small, cheap tests and let customers tell me what to build. My next step is to get a few pre-orders from the people I train with and see honestly whether they come back.
